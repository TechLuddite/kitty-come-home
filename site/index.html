KITTY COME HOME

Your indoor cat got out.

Here is what actually works, in the order to do it. Free, no account, works offline once loaded.

Kitty has not run away. Kitty is hiding, close, and may not answer you.

Indoor cats that escape bolt a short distance and then hide. The median distance from the door is 39 metres. Three quarters of cats are found within 500 m. A frightened cat's instinct is to stay hidden and still, so Kitty may well hear you and not come out, and that can last for days.

Keep calling. Just do not stop there. In the 2018 study 93% of owners called their cat's name while searching, and of those who got their cat back, only 34% said calling was what helped most, the lowest of any search method they measured. So keep doing it every time you go out, and do not treat silence as an answer. If you call and nothing happens, that tells you very little about whether Kitty is ten feet away.

This is the mistake that costs people their cats: they call, get silence, conclude Kitty is not there, and go and search somewhere else. Search as though Kitty cannot answer, and be glad if an answer comes.

Step 1 · right now

The first hour

Do these in order. Do not skip the first one, however certain you are that Kitty got out.

Step 2 · tonight, and every night this week

The night search

Do this every night. Walking the area at night with a torch was one of the four search methods that found cats fastest in the 2018 study. Practitioners add that a cat lying still through a noisy afternoon will often move once the street goes quiet, though that part is field experience rather than a measured finding.

The dark also gives you something daylight does not: eyeshine. A cat's eyes throw a torch beam straight back at you, from inside spaces you could never see into by day.

If you see eyeshine, do not rush at it. Read step 7 before you go out, not after.
Step 3 · before you sleep

Food and a camera

This is what keeps working while you sleep. It is also how you turn a guess into a confirmed location, which is what you need before setting a trap. Set it up tonight.

Do not put Kitty's litter tray outside. You will be told to. It is the most repeated piece of lost-cat advice there is and the Missing Animal Response Network, who are an author on the study this page is built from, specifically discourage it. Their objection is that the pheromones can attract territorial cats who then keep Kitty out of your own garden, and that no one has shown a cat can smell it from any distance. Other sources warn it can draw predators. The deeper problem is that it feels like doing something while you are not searching, and searching is the part with evidence behind it.
Expect a lot of clips, most of them wildlife, along with other cats and moving branches. Skim the 11pm to 5am window first and look for the low smooth movement and the eyeshine. Keep a week of footage: a clip that means nothing tonight means a lot after a sighting three streets away.
Night footage is greyscale, so colour tells you nothing. Judge on markings, pattern and proportion. Do not conclude from one bad clip either way. The common mistake is deciding a blurred grey shape is definitely not your cat, taking the station down, and losing the one thing that was working.
Step 4 · first thing tomorrow

The neighbours

11% of found cats were inside someone else's house or building, a garage, a shed, a basement. You cannot search those. Somebody has to ask, and that somebody is you.

This is the best-supported step on the page. In the 2018 study, "asked and received neighbours' permission to search their property using a slow methodical search" had the highest success of any specific search method, and speaking to neighbours was among the four fastest. Both beat every kind of advertising.

So knock, rather than posting a flyer through the door. What you actually need is for somebody to walk to the back of their garage and shine a light behind the freezer, and the study's finding is about owners who got permission and searched themselves.

What to say. "My indoor cat got out on Tuesday. Kitty is a grey tabby who has never been outside before, so Kitty will be hiding rather than wandering. Kitty often won't come out even after hearing you, so please don't take quiet as a no. Would you mind opening your shed and garage and having a proper look with a torch? Under the deck too. Here's a photo and my number, any time of night is fine, honestly."
Step 5 · tomorrow, once

Listings and shelters

These are listed after the physical search on purpose, and you should know why before you spend your evening on them.

The evidence here is genuinely uncomfortable. In the 2018 study, owners who advertised, or who contacted shelters and professionals, were less likely to get their cat back alive, and strongly so. The authors flag that these are simple associations that do not account for how long the cat had already been missing, which is the obvious explanation: people whose cats stay lost longer keep doing more of everything. Nobody has shown that advertising harms. What the study does not show is that it helps.

So do these once, properly, and then get back outside. Do not let a night of posting replace a night of searching. Of the advertising methods, fliers and posters came out best.

Under 2% of found cats were found in a shelter, which is the other reason this section is short.

Step 6 · from day one

Keeping track of where you looked

After two days every garden looks like one you already checked. People re-search the easy, well-lit half of the radius over and over and never once look in the culvert. Write it down. A notebook is enough.

Resist the urge to widen. As days pass the instinct is to search further out. The evidence says the opposite: search the same close ground more carefully, at a better hour, with a better torch.

Record three outcomes, not two

SearchedThe torch went in. You saw the whole space. Nothing there.
PartialYou looked but could not see all of it. Treat as unsearched. Go back.
BlockedLocked shed, no answer at the door. These are your best targets.

The blocked list matters more than the searched list. Those 11% of cats found in someone else's building were sitting on a blocked line in somebody's notebook until a person opened a door.

A place does not stay searched. Practitioners report that a displaced cat starts moving after the first day or two, and mostly at night, though that is field experience rather than a measured finding. Either way the cost of re-searching is low. Re-search ring 1 every night, at a different hour than last time. After any credible sighting, re-search everything within 50 m of it straight away.

Step 7 · read this before you need it

When you see Kitty

Eyeshine under a deck at 2am. Slow down before you do anything else. If Kitty bolts from here, the next hiding place may be somewhere you have not searched, and you would be starting again.

Do not assume Kitty will react to you in the usual way. The instinct is relief, then stepping forward, then a raised happy voice. Some cats do run straight to their owner. A frightened one in survival mode may read a fast approach as a predator, bolt, and relocate somewhere you have not searched. You cannot tell which you have got until you slow down, so slow down.

If Kitty will not come

Common, and not a bad sign. Do not force it. Leave food right there, back off out of sight, and note the exact spot. Move the feeding station and camera there tonight.

For a cat that is visible but will not approach, a humane trap is the usual next step. Be aware that the study found no evidence trapping improved the odds of getting a cat back (p = 0.307), so treat it as a tool for a cat you have already located rather than as a way to find one. Shelters, TNR groups and rescues will often lend or rent you a trap, and will give you local advice with it.

Set it where Kitty already feeds. Cover it with a blanket over the top and sides so it reads as a tunnel rather than a cage, put the food past the trip plate, and if you have a spare night, pre-bait it tied open first so Kitty learns the tunnel is safe.

Never leave a set trap unattended. This is the one rule everybody who does this agrees on. Alley Cat Allies say to watch traps from a distance and never walk away from them. Lost-pet trapping guidance allows up to three or four hours between checks in ordinary conditions, and says to check far more often, around hourly, in temperature extremes, meaning below freezing or above about 27C. A trapped animal cannot escape weather, a predator, or a person with bad intentions. If you cannot commit to checking, do not set it tonight.

When you catch Kitty: do not open the trap outside, not even for a moment. Cover it, carry it into one small closed room, shut the door, then open the trap and leave Kitty to come out alone. Expect Kitty to hide and be out of sorts for days. Get Kitty to a vet within a day or two even if nothing looks wrong.

If Kitty is somewhere you cannot reach, a wall cavity, under a building, up a tree, do not try to force Kitty out. No hose, no broom, no dog. Leave food at the opening, put the camera on it, and ask your shelter or a trained searcher.

Step 8

Do not give up at day seven

34% of cats are recovered by day 7. But 50% by day 30, and 61% within a year. A cat found at week six is an ordinary outcome, not a miracle. Displaced cats often stay hidden for days before they start moving and feeding, and that is exactly when the feeding station and the camera start earning their keep.

Keep the station stocked. Keep the night searches going. Keep going back to the neighbours.

Sources

Where these numbers come from

Almost everything here traces to one study: Huang, Coradini, Rand, Morton, Albrecht, Wasson and Robertson (2018), "Search Methods Used to Locate Missing Cats and Locations Where Missing Cats Are Found," Animals 8(1):5, a retrospective case series of 1,210 missing cats at the University of Queensland. doi.org/10.3390/ani8010005

The five-house radius figure and the behavioural model come from the Missing Animal Response Network, whose founder Kat Albrecht is an author on that study. Their case files are practice records rather than controlled research, and this page says so wherever it leans on them. The warning about litter trays is theirs too, and the trapping safety guidance comes from Alley Cat Allies and from published lost-pet trapping guidance.

What the study actually found about search methods

The four methods that got cats back fastest, in the study's own terms, were speaking to neighbours and asking them to look, walking the area at night with a torch, getting a neighbour's permission and searching their property slowly, and searching your own yard and immediate area. Asking permission and searching a neighbour's property had the highest success of any single method at 62%. Driving around had the worst outcome of any physical search method. That is why this page is ordered the way it is.

Where we are being careful

  • Physical searching did not reach statistical significance. The odds ratio was 1.49 with a confidence interval of 0.97 to 2.29, p = 0.071. That interval crosses 1, so this is a positive signal rather than proof. It is the strongest method signal in the study and it agrees with the distance findings and with practitioner experience, which is why we recommend it, but no single line of this is conclusive.
  • Advertising and contacting professionals were associated with worse outcomes, p < 0.001 for both. The authors state plainly that these associations are univariable and do not account for how long the cat had been missing, which almost certainly explains it. It is not evidence that advertising harms. It is a reason not to let it replace searching.
  • Trapping showed no detectable benefit (p = 0.307), and neither did identification devices (p = 0.331). The microchip result is not an argument against microchipping: a chip only helps at a scanner and under 2% of these cats reached one. Keep your cat chipped and keep the registration current.
  • The study did not separate indoor-only cats from cats with outdoor experience when it analysed search methods, and says so in its own limitations. Only the distance figures are broken out for indoor-only cats. So the 39 m is specific to cats like yours; the advice about which methods work is drawn from all cats in the study.
  • The behavioural model is practitioner experience, not a controlled finding. Where a step on this page rests on it rather than on the study, the page says so.
  • Recall is a real weakness. 71% of respondents were describing a cat that had gone missing more than six months earlier, with a median of about two years. The authors also note the sample was likely more attached to their cats, and more willing to search hard, than owners in general.

The full evidence write-up, including every caveat and the working, is in the project repository.

If something here is wrong, please tell us. If you do this work (shelter or rescue staff, TNR trappers, trained searchers) or you found your own cat and learned something the hard way, please open an issue. Field experience counts as evidence. This is the part where being wrong has a real cost.
