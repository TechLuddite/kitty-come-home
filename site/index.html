KITTY COME HOME

Your indoor cat got out.

Here is what actually works, in the order to do it. Free, no account, works offline once loaded.

She has not run away. She is hiding, close, and she may not answer you.

Indoor cats that escape bolt a short distance and then hide. The median distance from the door is 39 metres. Three quarters of cats are found within 500 m. A frightened cat's instinct is to stay hidden and still, so she may well hear you and not come out, and that can last for days.

Keep calling. Just do not stop there. Calling her name is one of the tactics owners reported alongside getting their cat back, so it is worth doing every time you go out. What it is not is a test. If you call and nothing happens, that tells you almost nothing about whether she is ten feet away.

This is the mistake that costs people their cats: they call, get silence, conclude she is not there, and go and search somewhere else. Search as though she cannot answer, and be glad when she does.

Step 1 · right now

The first hour

Do these in order. Do not skip the first one, however certain you are that she got out.

Step 2 · tonight, and every night this week

The night search

This is the single most valuable thing you will do. A cat that will not move at 2pm with traffic and dogs about will often shift position at 2am. And in the dark you get an advantage you do not have in daylight: eyeshine. A cat's eyes throw a torch beam back brilliantly from a long way off, from inside a space you could never see into by day.

If you see eyeshine, do not rush at it. Read step 7 before you go out, not after.
Step 3 · before you sleep

Food and a camera

This is what searches while you sleep, and over a long search it is where most confirmed sightings come from. Set it up tonight.

Expect hundreds of clips a night, nearly all raccoons, opossums, other cats and moving branches. Skim the 11pm to 5am window first and look for the low smooth movement and the eyeshine. Keep a week of footage: a clip that means nothing tonight means a lot after a sighting three streets away.
Night footage is greyscale, so colour tells you nothing. Judge on markings, pattern and proportion. Do not conclude from one bad clip either way. The common mistake is deciding a blurred grey shape is definitely not your cat, taking the station down, and losing the one thing that was working.
Step 4 · first thing tomorrow

The neighbours

11% of found cats were inside someone else's house or building, a garage, a shed, a basement. You cannot search those. Somebody has to ask, and that somebody is you.

Knock. A flyer through the door produces sympathy; what you need is for a person to walk to the back of their garage and shine a light behind the freezer. Almost nobody does that because of a leaflet. A fair number will do it because someone stood at their door and asked.

What to say. "My indoor cat got out on Tuesday. She's a grey tabby, and she's never been outside before, so she'll be hiding rather than wandering. She often won't come out even if she hears you, so please don't take quiet as a no. Would you mind opening your shed and garage and having a proper look with a torch? Under the deck too. Here's a photo and my number, any time of night is fine, honestly."
Step 5 · tomorrow, once

Listings and shelters

These matter, and they are deliberately listed after the physical search. Under 2% of found cats were found in a shelter. Do these once, properly, then get back outside.

Step 6 · from day one

Keeping track of where you looked

After two days every garden looks like one you already checked. People re-search the easy, well-lit half of the radius over and over and never once look in the culvert. Write it down. A notebook is enough.

Resist the urge to widen. As days pass the instinct is to search further out. The evidence says the opposite: search the same close ground more carefully, at a better hour, with a better torch.

Record three outcomes, not two

SearchedThe torch went in. You saw the whole space. Nothing there.
PartialYou looked but could not see all of it. Treat as unsearched. Go back.
BlockedLocked shed, no answer at the door. These are your best targets.

The blocked list matters more than the searched list. Those 11% of cats found in someone else's building were sitting on a blocked line in somebody's notebook until a person opened a door.

A place does not stay searched. She moves, usually at night, usually after the first day or two of lying still. Re-search ring 1 every night, and at a different hour than last time. After any credible sighting, re-search everything within 50 m of it immediately.

Step 7 · read this before you need it

When you see her

Eyeshine under a deck at 2am. What you do in the next sixty seconds decides whether this ends tonight or runs another week.

Do not assume she will react to you the way she normally does. The instinct is relief, then stepping forward, then a raised happy voice. Some cats do run straight to their owner. A frightened one in survival mode may read a fast approach as a predator, bolt, and relocate somewhere you have not searched. You cannot tell which you have got until you slow down, so slow down.

If she will not come

Common, and not a bad sign. Do not force it. Leave food right there, back off out of sight, and note the exact spot. Move the feeding station and camera there tonight. For a cat that is visible but unapproachable, a humane trap is usually the answer, and pushing the approach instead is usually what loses her.

Borrow a trap: shelters, TNR groups and rescues commonly lend them free. Set it where she already feeds, cover it with a blanket top and sides so it reads as a tunnel, put the food past the trip plate, and pre-bait it tied open for a night first if you can.

Never leave a set trap unattended. Check it at least hourly, through the night. A trapped animal cannot escape weather, a predator, or someone with bad intentions. If you cannot commit to checking hourly, do not set it tonight.

When you catch her: do not open the trap outside, not even for a moment. Cover it, carry it into one small closed room, shut the door, then open the trap and leave her to come out alone. Expect her to hide and be unlike herself for days. Get her to a vet within a day or two even if she looks fine.

If she is somewhere you cannot reach, a wall cavity, under a building, up a tree, do not try to force her out. No hose, no broom, no dog. Leave food at the opening, put the camera on it, and ask your shelter or a trained searcher.

Step 8

Do not give up at day seven

34% of cats are recovered by day 7. But 50% by day 30, and 61% within a year. A cat found at week six is an ordinary outcome, not a miracle. Displaced cats often stay hidden for days before they start moving and feeding, and that is exactly when the feeding station and the camera start earning their keep.

Keep the station stocked. Keep the night searches going. Keep going back to the neighbours.

Sources

Where these numbers come from

Almost everything here traces to one study: Huang, Coradini, Rand, Morton, Albrecht, Wasson and Robertson (2018), "Search Methods Used to Locate Missing Cats and Locations Where Missing Cats Are Found," Animals 8(1):5, a retrospective case series of 1,210 missing cats at the University of Queensland. doi.org/10.3390/ani8010005

The five-house radius figure and the behavioural model come from the Missing Animal Response Network, whose founder Kat Albrecht is an author on that study.

Where we are being careful

  • Physical searching reached p = 0.071. That is the strongest method signal in the study and it does not clear the conventional 0.05 threshold. We recommend it because three independent lines of evidence agree, not because any one is conclusive.
  • The behavioural model is practitioner experience, not a controlled finding. It is consistent with the distance data, and several steps here depend on it.
  • Microchipping showed no detectable effect in this data (p = 0.331). That is not an argument against microchips. A chip only helps at a scanner, and under 2% of these cats reached one. Keep your cat chipped.
  • The sample was self-selected and largely suburban. Dense urban and rural settings may behave differently.

The full evidence write-up, including every caveat and the working, is in the project repository.

If something here is wrong, please tell us. If you do this work (shelter or rescue staff, TNR trappers, trained searchers) or you found your own cat and learned something the hard way, please open an issue. Field experience counts as evidence. This is the part where being wrong has a real cost.
