# The first 24 hours

**If your indoor cat just got out, start here. Read section 1 now and the rest when you have a
minute. It is 11pm and you are frightened, that is the normal condition for reading this.**

The single most useful thing to know before you do anything else:

> Your cat has almost certainly not run away. Indoor cats that get out bolt a short distance and
> then hide, and stay hidden. The median distance from the door is about **39 metres**. Three
> quarters of cats are found within 500 m. The cat is very probably within a few houses of you
> right now, and may be too frightened to answer.

**Keep calling.** Calling the cat's name is one of the tactics owners reported alongside getting
their cat back, and plenty of cats do come out. Whether yours does depends on the cat, on what has
happened to it recently, on how long it has been out, and on who is calling and how.

What calling is not is a test. This is the mistake that costs people their cats: you call, nothing
happens, you conclude it is not there, and you go and search somewhere else. A frightened cat in
survival mode can sit ten feet away and stay silent, sometimes for days.

So: **search as though it cannot answer, and be glad when it does.** You are looking for an animal
wedged into a small dark space as well as calling a pet in for dinner, and the searching is the
part people skip.

---

## 1. Right now: the first hour

Do these in order. Do not skip step 1, however sure you are.

1. **Search your own home first. Completely.** Of the cats found in the 2018 study, 4% were
   inside their own home. Of those, a third were behind or under furniture, a third were in the
   basement, and one was inside the box springs of a couch. Check inside
   sofa bases and box springs, behind and under every appliance, inside cupboards, wardrobes,
   drawers, the back of closets, the dryer, the dishwasher, any suitcase or box, the loft, the
   basement, the garage, and anywhere a cat could have been shut in when a door closed. Move
   things. Look with a torch. A cat can flatten into a space you would swear was solid.

2. **Work out how it got out, and mark the spot.** The open door, the broken screen, the gap
   behind a vent, the window that does not latch. This is the worst minute of the night and it
   is also the most useful one, because everything else in this document is measured from that
   spot: the feeding station goes there, the camera goes there, and the search rings are drawn
   around it. If you genuinely cannot tell, use the most likely door and adjust when you learn
   more.

3. **Prop your door open if you safely can**, or leave a window cracked with a screen removed at
   ground level. Cats often return on their own during the quiet hours before dawn. Put the
   a light on and listen out: 18% of the cats found outside in the 2018 study were found right at the
   entrance to their own home.

   **Do not put the litter tray outside.** You will be told to, by almost everyone. The Missing Animal
   Response Network, an author on the study this protocol is built from, specifically discourages it:
   the pheromones can attract territorial cats who then keep Kitty out of your own garden, nobody has
   shown a cat can smell it from any distance, and other sources warn it can draw predators. Its worst
   effect is that it feels like doing something instead of searching.

4. **Get the whole household quiet and switch off the noise.** TV off, music off. You are
   listening for a very faint sound: a rustle, a bell, a single short cry.

5. **Go outside and stand still for two minutes before you search anything.** Listen. This
   sounds like wasted time and is not.

6. **Search the ground immediately against your own house first**, under the deck or porch, in
   the crawlspace, under the steps, in the shrubs against the wall, under the parked cars in your
   own driveway, in the shed, in the garage, behind the bins. A cat that bolts out a door
   frequently does not get past its own property line.

7. **Then work outward on foot.** Do not drive. You cannot see under a hedge at 20 mph, and the
   cat is 39 metres away, not 3 kilometres.

## 2. Tonight: the night search

**This is the highest-value single activity in the whole protocol. Do it tonight, and again
tomorrow night.**

Cats are most likely to move in the quiet hours. A hidden cat that will not move at 2pm with
traffic and dogs about will often shift position at 2am. And in the dark you have an advantage
you do not have in daylight: **eyeshine**. A cat's eyes throw back a torch beam brilliantly from
a long way off, from inside a space you could never see into by day.

1. Go out between roughly **midnight and 4am**, when the street is at its quietest.
2. Take a **bright torch**, a proper one, not a phone. Hold it **at eye level, next to your
   temple**. The reflection only comes back along the same line it went out, so a torch held low
   at your hip will not show you anything.
3. Move **slowly** and sweep the beam low and level: under decks, under cars, under sheds, into
   drains and culverts, into hedge bases, into woodpiles, under porches, through crawlspace
   vents, into gaps under fences.
4. **Stop every 20 metres or so.** Stand still. Be quiet for a full minute. Then move on.
5. When you call, use the tone you use at home for something good, food, a treat, the normal
   soft voice. Not the alarmed voice. Shake a treat bag or tap a food tin. Then **be silent and
   listen for thirty seconds** before making another sound.
6. If you see eyeshine, **do not rush at it**. See [`approach-and-recovery.md`](approach-and-recovery.md).

Go slowly enough that it feels absurd. A proper night search of a five-house radius takes over an
hour. That is correct.

## 3. Tonight: set up a feeding station

Before you go to bed, set up a feeding station at the point the cat escaped from, and a camera
if you have anything at all that will do.

This is what works while you sleep, and it is how most of the confirmed sightings in a long
search arrive. Full detail: [`feeding-station-and-camera.md`](feeding-station-and-camera.md).

## 4. Tomorrow morning: the neighbours

11% of found cats were inside someone else's house or building, a garage, a shed, a basement, a
garden room. **You cannot search those. Someone has to ask, and that someone is you.**

Knock on doors. Not a flyer through the letterbox, an actual conversation, because what you need
is for them to go and physically open their own shed and look, and a leaflet will not make that
happen. Full script and coverage plan: [`neighbor-canvass.md`](neighbor-canvass.md).

Prioritise the properties nearest the escape point and work outward. Five houses in every
direction covers the great majority of cases.

## 5. Tomorrow: the administrative layer

These matter, but they are second-order for an indoor cat, and they are listed after the physical
search deliberately. Under 2% of found cats were in a shelter. Do these once, properly, then get
back outside.

1. **File on [Petco Love Lost](https://lost.petcolove.org/)**, free, national, has photo
   matching, and most California shelters use it. Upload several clear photos.
2. **Post to [PawBoost](https://www.pawboost.com/)**, free to post, wide social reach.
3. **Post in your local lost-pet and neighbourhood Facebook groups, Nextdoor, and Ring
   Neighbors.** These are where nearby sightings actually surface.
4. **Call and then physically visit your local shelters.** Visit in person and walk the kennels
   yourself, descriptions get logged wrong, and a "grey tabby" in the system may be your cat.
   Repeat every few days. California requires a stray cat be held six business days (four under
   some conditions), not counting the day of impoundment, so a visit within that window matters.
5. **Confirm your microchip registration** is current with your correct phone number.
6. **Call local vets and rescues**, an injured found cat goes to a vet, not a shelter.
7. **Put up signs.** Large, few words, readable from a car: LOST CAT, a big photo, your phone
   number. Corners and junctions, not mid-block.

## 6. What to keep doing

- **Night search, every night, for at least the first week.** The recovery curve is steep early,
  34% by day 7, and this is the activity with the best evidence behind it.
- **Check the camera every morning.**
- **Re-canvass the neighbours after three or four days.** People who said no the first time will
  have opened the shed since, and some will have started leaving food out for a cat they assumed
  was a stray.
- **Widen slowly.** Do not abandon the close radius to search further out. If the first sweep
  found nothing, the answer is usually to search the same ground *better*, not to search
  different ground.

## 7. Do not give up at day seven

Only 34% are recovered by day 7, but 50% by day 30, and 61% within a year. A cat found at week
six is an ordinary outcome, not a miracle. Displaced cats often stay hidden for days before they
start moving and feeding, and that is when the feeding station and the camera start earning their
keep.

Keep the station stocked. Keep checking. Keep asking the neighbours.

---

## The short version

If you remember nothing else:

1. Search your own house completely.
2. Search on foot, not from a car, within five houses.
3. Search at 2am with a torch held at eye level.
4. Knock on doors and ask people to open their sheds.
5. Put food and a camera where it got out.
6. It is closer than you think, and silence does not mean it is not there.
